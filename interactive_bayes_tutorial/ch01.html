    What’s your prior

    How it works

    After h heads and t tails, a Beta(α, β) prior updates to Beta(α + h, β + t). The posterior mean is (α + h)/(α + β + h + t). The weak and strong panels share a density scale. The dotted vertical line marks θ = 0.5.

    Read the purple likelihood curve using the right axis. The relative likelihood divides θh(1 − θ)t by its maximum. All three panels use the same likelihood because they use the same tosses. The starting counts (14 heads in 17 tosses) come from the STAT 6206 slides, pp. 17–20. The order shown is illustrative. See also Seeing Theory’s Prior to Posterior experiment.

    How the Posterior Moves with More Data

    Play sample growth to see the posterior for a Normal mean narrow and move toward the sample mean. Only the sample size changes during playback. The observation standard deviation is fixed at 2.

    The posterior precision is the prior precision plus the sample precision. The posterior mean weights the two means by their precisions. STAT 6206 Chapter 1, p. 29.

    The Same Prior Looks Different on Different Scales

    A uniform prior on the success probability θ looks completely flat.
    But when we reparameterize it to the log-odds scale γ = log[θ / (1 − θ)], the same prior becomes a logistic density that peaks at θ = 0.5.

    Drag the cutoff to see that the shaded probability is identical on both scales — only the visual shape changes.

    Key takeaway: There is no unique “non-informative” prior. A prior that looks uninformative on one scale may be informative on another.

    γ = log[θ/(1 − θ)] and |dθ/dγ| = θ(1 − θ). The log-odds plot shows −8 to 8. The reported probability includes the area below −8. STAT 6206 Chapter 1, p. 21.
